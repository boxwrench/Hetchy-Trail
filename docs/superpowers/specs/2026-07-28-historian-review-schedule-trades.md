# Historian Review — Schedule-for-Resilience Choices

**For:** the reviewing historian.
**From:** game design. Nothing in `content/` or `data/events/` has been changed.
No card has been edited, and none will be until this review comes back.
**Date:** 2026-07-28

---

## What is being asked

Several cards in the game let the player choose to protect the project —
its money, its workforce, its public standing, its eventual capacity — at the
cost of falling behind schedule. On seven of the fifteen multiple-choice cards
that trade already exists. On the rest it does not: the protective option is
either the same speed or actually faster.

Where a card lacks it, we would like to know whether it *could* honestly have it:
whether the record supports the idea that this particular decision could have
been made more slowly and more carefully.

**Design proposes nothing on its own authority here.** Each card below sets out
what the record says, what a slower option would be, what it would teach, and
where it could mislead. The last line of each is blank for your ruling.

Two cards are included that need no change at all. They are the models the
others are measured against, and they are here so you can see the pattern being
asked about rather than take our word for it.

## How to read the ratings

Two separate judgements, deliberately not combined:

**Design value** — how much the game gains. Design's call.

| | |
|---|---|
| **High** | The card currently offers no real trade; adding one changes what the player is deciding. |
| **Medium** | A trade exists but is weak, or the gain is modest. |
| **Low** | Little or nothing gained. |

**Historical support** — how well the record backs the proposed slower option.
**Your call, and it governs.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Documented** | The record states or directly implies it. |
| **Inferred** | Consistent with the record and ordinary practice, but not stated. |
| **Speculative** | Plausible, but the record is silent. |

A card can be high design value and speculative support. That combination is a
*reject* unless you say otherwise — which is the whole reason the axes are
split. An earlier draft of this document merged them into one "strong candidate"
label, and that label was quietly doing your job for you.

## Ground rules used

Applied to each card independently. No target number of changes was set, and no
desired difficulty was aimed at.

1. **The record governs.** A slower option is proposed only where the historical
   material supports it.
2. **No invented events.** Nothing proposed adds an incident, decision, or
   outcome that did not occur or was not genuinely under discussion.
3. **The canonical choice does not move.** What actually happened stays marked as
   what actually happened. Only the alternative is under discussion.
4. **Deviations get recorded.** Anything approved gets a line in that card's
   assumption note, per the project's standing rule.

## Two corrections to our own count

Recorded because they affect what this document claims, and because both were
found by challenge rather than by us.

**First**, an earlier count of the cards that trade schedule for resilience
scored only funds, public support, and crew wellbeing — omitting water
readiness. That hid **Gravity or Pumps?**, where two extra seasons buy capacity.

**Second**, and more consequentially, that count treated protection as *gaining*
a metric. But declining to lose one is equally protection. That hid **Mud, Bay
and Bridge Finance**, where refusing the Spring Valley advance holds public
support level while accepting it costs a point.

Corrected, **seven of fifteen** multiple-choice cards already carry the trade,
not five. The problem is narrower than first stated: the pattern is established
in the deck, and the question is whether a few specific cards that invert it
should stop doing so.

## An open design question that this review does not settle

Two of the candidates below — **Cut the First Road** and **Six Camps in the
Foothills** — currently give the thorough option a *time bonus*, and that bonus
is not a mistake. Preparation genuinely prevented later interruption: a real
road and a working sawmill made everything downstream faster.

So the game's single time number is being asked to mean two different things:

- **Upfront duration** — how long this decision takes now.
- **Net campaign effect** — what it does to the project's total schedule.

Today those cards encode the second. Simply reversing the sign would erase a true
lesson and replace it with a false one.

There is evidence about the original intent. The Cut the First Road assumption
note records that the brief specified *"Time -1 on two later High Sierra cards"*,
consolidated during implementation into a single immediate gain. The benefit was
meant to be deferred, and collapsing it forward is what produced a card where
preparing is instantly faster.

**This is design's decision, not yours.** The honest resolution is likely to be
that the time number means upfront duration, with the later benefit carried by
the campaign flags these cards already grant — so preparation costs a season now
and pays it back later, as it did.

What is useful from you on those two cards is narrower and purely factual:
**did the preparation take time before heavy work could begin?** Not how the game
should score it.

# Reference models

## 15 — `gravity_or_pumps` — Gravity or Pumps?

**Historical decision actually available.** Whether to build the 28.5-mile
gravity tunnel through the Coast Range or the discussed pumped pipeline
alternative. Construction was delayed until 1927; the gravity tunnel offered
greater capacity without permanent pumping costs.

**The trade, as already implemented.** The gravity tunnel costs two extra seasons
and more money. It buys capacity and freedom from perpetual pumping bills.

**Metric protected.** Water readiness, and the city's long-term operating costs.

**What it teaches.** The right answer took longer, and the city took it anyway.

**Recommendation.** No change. Included so the pattern under discussion is
visible in a card you can check against the record.

## 20 — `mud_bay_and_bridge_finance` — Mud, Bay and Bridge Finance

**Historical decision actually available.** Whether to accept the Spring Valley
Water Company's advance when city funds were exhausted. The city accepted; the
advance helped prevent a prolonged shutdown and layoffs.

**The trade, as already implemented.** Accepting the advance costs a point of
public support. Refusing holds support level — and costs four seasons relative to
accepting, plus funds and crew while the crews scatter and are reassembled.
Refusal is expensive, but it protects something, and the something it protects is
the municipal-independence principle the whole project was fought over.

*An earlier draft of this document described refusal as strictly worse in every
respect. That was wrong: it read protection as gaining a metric rather than
declining to lose one.*

**Metric protected.** Public support.

**What it teaches.** That municipal ownership had a price, and that the city
weighed paying it.

**Recommendation.** **Reference model — no change needed.**

**Optional strengthening, if you think the record supports it.** Refusal
currently *avoids* a loss. It could instead *gain* support — the city visibly
holding its independence — which would make the tension more legible to a player
who never sees the comparison. Design value: medium. Historical support: design
reads this as **speculative**, since the record documents what the advance
prevented rather than any public reaction to a refusal that did not happen.
Design does not press for it.

**Historian ruling:**

# Candidates

## 12 — `room_for_four_pipes` — Leave Room for Four Pipes

**Historical decision actually available.** How wide a right-of-way to acquire
between the Oakdale and Tesla portals, when only Pipeline No. 1 was to be built.
The city bought a corridor about 100 feet wide, with room for as many as four
parallel San Joaquin pipelines.

**Proposed slower alternative.** The wider corridor takes longer to assemble —
more owners, more parcels, more negotiation — so the far-sighted purchase costs a
season the narrow strip does not.

**Metric protected.** Future capacity (recorded as the `row_expansion_secured`
flag) and water readiness.

**Plausible schedule consequence.** One season, spent acquiring land rather than
building.

**Supporting source.** SFPUC historical materials as summarised in the mapping
brief, section 5: a right-of-way approximately 100 feet wide, providing room for
as many as four parallel pipelines.

**What the choice would teach.** That the decision which made San Francisco's
later pipelines possible was paid for twice — in money, and in time nobody could
point to on a map.

**Risk of distorting the record.** The proposal treats a longer negotiation for
more parcels as ordinary practice. The summarised record does not say acquisition
delayed anything.

**Design value: High.** This card currently has no time dimension at all — both
options are instantaneous — so this adds a decision rather than reweighting one.

**Historical support: design reads this as inferred.** Your ruling governs. If
you read it as speculative, design withdraws the proposal.

**Historian ruling:**

## 01 — `cut_the_first_road` — Cut the First Road

**Historical decision actually available.** How much permanent access and timber
supply to build before heavy construction began. Road work started around Hog
Ranch in 1914; the Canyon Ranch sawmill began operating in July 1915, about 4.5
miles from the dam site.

**Proposed slower alternative.** Completing the road and getting the sawmill into
production before heavy hauling begins costs a season up front — with the benefit
arriving later, through the access flag this card already grants, rather than as
an immediate speed bonus.

**Metric protected.** Crew wellbeing, and funds across later High Sierra work.

**Plausible schedule consequence.** One season before dam work opens.

**Supporting source.** Mapping brief, section 5: road work from 1914, sawmill
operating July 1915.

**What the choice would teach.** That nothing visible happens in the first year of
a twenty-year project, and that this is the reason the rest of it worked.

**Risk of distorting the record.** Low as to fact. The risk is mechanical rather
than historical, and is the design question set out above: the current time bonus
encodes a true net effect, and must be re-expressed rather than deleted.

**Design value: High** — but contingent. If the time number keeps meaning net
campaign effect, this proposal is wrong and should be dropped.

**Historical support: design reads this as documented** — roughly a year between
starting access and having timber.

**What design needs from you, specifically:** only whether preparation took time
before heavy work could begin. The scoring question is ours.

**Historian ruling:**

## 09 — `six_camps_in_the_foothills` — Six Camps in the Foothills

**Historical decision actually available.** How much camp infrastructure to
provide before distributing tunnelling crews. Six camps supported Foothill Tunnel
work; city crews installed water, electricity, telephones and roads *before and
during* excavation.

**Proposed slower alternative.** Servicing all six camps before the crews spread
out — rather than while they work — costs a season and holds the workforce.

**Metric protected.** Crew wellbeing.

**Plausible schedule consequence.** One season before excavation begins.

**Supporting source.** Mapping brief, section 5: six camps, with support
facilities installed before and during excavation.

**What the choice would teach.** That worker conditions were a scheduling
decision, not a courtesy — and that the alternative, crews drifting away, cost
more time than the camps did.

**Risk of distorting the record.** The record explicitly allows installation
*during* excavation, so charging a full season for doing it first overstates the
sequencing. This is weaker ground than Cut the First Road.

**Design value: High**, contingent on the same design question.

**Historical support: design reads this as inferred**, because "before and
during" supports preparation taking time without establishing that it delayed
anything.

**Historian ruling:**

## 18 — `sell_the_bonds_finish_the_bore` — Sell the Bonds, Finish the Bore

**Historical decision actually available.** How to respond when financial
shortages suspended portions of tunnel work. A 1932 bond issue provided
additional money; the last Coast Range Tunnel connection was made on January 5,
1934, between the Mitchell and Mocho headings.

**Proposed slower alternative.** Campaigning a further season before going to the
voters — a better-prepared bond campaign that protects public support rather than
risking the vote.

**Metric protected.** Public support, and the security of the bond issue.

**Plausible schedule consequence.** One season of campaigning before the vote.

**Supporting source.** Mapping brief, section 5: shortages suspended work; the
1932 issue passed.

**What the choice would teach.** That the project's finance was political, and
persuading the city was work in its own right.

**Risk of distorting the record.** The record documents that the bonds passed,
not how the campaign was paced. This card already carries one counterfactual
branch; this would add a second to a card that is already partly invented.

**Design value: Medium.** The card already has a slow option, and this would
mostly make it less punishing.

**Historical support: design reads this as speculative.** On design's own ground
rules that is a reject unless you say the record supports it.

# Rejections

## 16 — `crane_ridge_closes_in` — Crane Ridge Closes In

**Historical decision actually available.** How to respond when swelling ground
began crushing timber supports about 2,500 feet down — in one reported section an
approximately 18-foot opening contracted to about three feet within 24 hours.
Crews excavated an oversized bore and built gunite support rings with room for
the ground to move.

**Why this is now a rejection.** An earlier draft called it *possible*, proposing
to widen the time gap so caution cost more. That does not survive its own
constraint. The record is clear that gunite worked and timber caused
re-excavation, so gunite must not cost more total time than the timber failure —
and the card already prices it correctly at one season against two. Under the
present data model, where a card carries a single net time number, this card
cannot honestly become a schedule-for-resilience trade. Pausing for gunite
prevents *greater* delay. That is what happened, and the card already says so.

**Design value: Low.** **Historical support: documented — and it forbids the
change.**

**Recommendation.** **Reject.**

**Historian ruling:**

## 10 — `red_mountain_bar` — Beat the Rising Reservoir

**Historical decision actually available.** Whether to complete the 770-foot
steel inverted siphon before the filling of Don Pedro Reservoir made the canyon
harder to reach.

**Proposed slower alternative.** None. The card's lesson is that some deadlines
are not the project's to negotiate, and a "take it slowly and stay safe" option
would undercut exactly that.

**Supporting source.** Mapping brief, section 5: the crossing was necessary
because the canyon would be affected by the filling of Don Pedro Reservoir.

**Recommendation.** **Reject.**

*Separate note for design, not requiring a historian ruling:* the delay option
here protects nothing — it costs more money, more time, and gains no metric. It
is a choice no informed player would take. Because the card's lesson is that the
deadline is external, the fix is to remove or reframe the false choice rather
than to make delay rewarding.

**Historian ruling (not required):**

## 17 — `mitchell_shaft_memorial` — Mitchell Shaft Memorial

**Historical decision actually available.** How thoroughly to review ventilation
and gas monitoring after the July 17, 1931 explosion at Mitchell Shaft, which
killed twelve workers. Investigations documented existing safety precautions but
did not establish one conclusive cause.

**Proposed slower alternative.** None recommended.

**Why.** Mechanically, charging schedule for a full stand-down would teach that
thorough investigation of a fatal accident is a cost the player may decline. A
player who notices the brief review is cheaper has been taught something this
project should not teach. The card is currently built so the loss falls on both
options and the stand-down partially offsets it. That reads as deliberate, and
design believes it is right.

**Recommendation.** **Reject.** Design does not recommend changing this card and
will not act on its own judgement here. Reopen only if you specifically direct
it — and if so, the brief-review option must not become mechanically attractive.

**Historian ruling:**

## 21 — `first_drop_at_pulgas` — The First Drop at Pulgas

**Historical decision actually available.** Whether to mark the arrival of the
first Hetch Hetchy water at Pulgas — 10:12 a.m., October 24, 1934 — with a public
celebration. One followed on October 28.

**Proposed slower alternative.** None. The celebration took four days; the game's
smallest unit of time is a season. This is also the final card of the campaign,
so any time cost would be mechanically inert as well as historically overstated.

**Design value: Low.**

**Recommendation.** **Reject.**
